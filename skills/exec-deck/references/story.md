# Story: why the exec-deck workflow is shaped this way
Paraphrased from the sources at the end (read 2026-11-23; the books through their tables of contents and summaries, not in full).

## Context first (Knaflic)
Before any chart, settle three things: **who** the audience is and what they need, **what** you want them to know or do, and **how** you will deliver it (live, sent ahead, read alone). Two tools force clarity:
- **3-minute story**: if you had only three minutes and no slides, what would you say? If you cannot say it, the analysis is not ready to present.
- **Big Idea**: one complete sentence with your point of view and what is at stake. It is the line you want the audience to remember.
Then **storyboard** the flow before opening any slide tool.

Knaflic's six lessons, in order: understand the context; choose an appropriate visual; eliminate clutter; focus attention where you want it; think like a designer; tell a story. A story has a beginning (the situation and why it matters), a middle (the evidence and what could happen) and an end (the call to action). Repetition helps: say what you will tell them, tell them, then say what you told them.

## Answer first (Minto)
Executives read top-down. The **pyramid principle** puts the governing thought, which is the answer or recommendation, at the top; supporting arguments below it; evidence at the base. Arguments in a group are **MECE**: they do not overlap and together leave no gap.

The opening follows **SCQA**:
- **Situation**: what the audience already knows and agrees with.
- **Complication**: what changed or what is wrong.
- **Question**: the question the complication raises.
- **Answer**: your recommendation, which is the top of the pyramid.

## Action titles and the ghost deck (consulting practice derived from Minto)
Each slide carries one message, stated in its title as a full sentence. Reading only the titles, in order, should tell the whole story; that also protects a deck when someone flips to one slide out of order. Writing the titles first, before any chart (the "ghost deck"), tests the storyline when it is still cheap to change.

## Data, narrative and visuals (Dykes)
A data story needs all three: data alone does not persuade, narrative without data is an opinion, and visuals without narrative leave the audience to find the point. A common arc: set the scene (the current situation), build up through supporting insights, reach the central insight (the "aha"), then end with the recommendation and next steps. Remove or aggregate secondary data points that compete with the message.

## Author-driven, not reader-driven (Segel & Heer)
Narrative visualizations range from author-driven (a fixed order, heavy messaging, little interaction) to reader-driven (free exploration). A live executive deck sits at the author-driven end: you choose the order and say what each view means. Exploration belongs in the appendix or in a separate dashboard.
