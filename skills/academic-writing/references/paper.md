# Paper: structure, sentences and the submission checklist
Paraphrased from the sources at the end (read 2026-11-23; the rule titles of Mensh & Kording, the maxims of Gopen & Swan and the seven suggestions of Peyton Jones checked against the original texts that day).

## One message, context–content–conclusion (Mensh & Kording 2017)
Readers want the main idea quickly; reviewers want to check that the conclusions follow; editors want significance. The ten rules, paraphrased:
1. Focus the paper on one central contribution, and say it in the title.
2. Write for real people who do not know your work: define terms, avoid abbreviations.
3. Use context–content–conclusion at every scale: paper, section, paragraph.
4. Keep the logical flow: avoid zig-zagging between topics; use parallel structure for parallel ideas; keep one term for one idea.
5. The abstract tells the whole story: context, the gap, "here we…", the main results, what they mean.
6. The introduction says why the paper matters: the gap it fills.
7. The results are a sequence of statements, each supported by figures, that connect logically to the central contribution.
8. The discussion says how the gap was filled, the limitations, and the relevance beyond the paper.
9. Spend the time where readers do: title, abstract, figures, outline.
10. Get feedback early and use it to reduce, reuse and recycle the story.

## Reader expectations in sentences (Gopen & Swan 1990)
Readers interpret text by where information sits. Seven maxims, paraphrased:
1. Put the grammatical subject's verb right after the subject.
2. Put the new information you want to emphasize at the end of the sentence (the stress position).
3. Put the person or thing whose story the sentence tells at the beginning (the topic position).
4. Put old, linking information at the beginning, to connect with what came before.
5. State the action of each clause in its verb, not in a noun.
6. Give the context before asking the reader to take in something new.
7. Make the structure emphasize what the substance emphasizes.

## Seven suggestions for papers (Peyton Jones)
Paraphrased from his talk: don't wait, write (writing clarifies the research); identify your key idea and say it clearly; tell a story; state your contributions explicitly, as claims a reader can check; put related work after your idea, not before it; put readers first, with examples before the general case; get feedback from readers who do not know the work.

## Submission checklist
Adapted from the NeurIPS paper checklist (partial; check the venue's current one) and the urutau method rules on evidence and reporting.
- [ ] The claims in the abstract and introduction match the contributions and the results; aspirational goals are marked as such.
- [ ] Limitations are discussed: where the results do not apply, what was not tested, what could break them.
- [ ] Theoretical results state their assumptions and give complete proofs (or say where).
- [ ] Experiments can be reproduced from the paper: data, splits, settings, number of runs, compute.
- [ ] Data and code availability is stated.
- [ ] Results report uncertainty (intervals or spread over runs or cases) and say how it was computed.
- [ ] The baseline was reproduced in the same setup and given the same tuning effort as the new method.
- [ ] No claim of "no effect" without a test showing the effect is smaller than the smallest that matters.
- [ ] Every number in the text traces to a script or output (`claims.md`).
- [ ] The venue's format, length and anonymity rules are met.
