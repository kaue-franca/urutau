# Talk: assertion–evidence slides and a talk built from the claims
Paraphrased from the sources at the end (read 2026-11-23).

## Assertion–evidence (Alley and Garner)
Each slide has a **sentence headline** that states its main message (the assertion) and a **body of visual evidence** that supports it (a figure, diagram, photo or equation), with text only where the visual needs it. The common default it replaces is a topic phrase plus a bulleted list.

How strong the evidence for it is:
- Garner & Alley (2013), 110 engineering students: the assertion–evidence group showed better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and better recall at a delayed test.
- Wolfe, Alley & Garner (2012), about 100 participants: only a positive trend for complex concepts, not statistically significant.
- So: plausible and consistent with multimedia-learning theory, but the studies are few and mostly from one group. Use it as the default, not as a proven law.

## Slide design (Naegle 2021)
One major idea per slide and about one minute per slide. Headings are read first, so they carry the point. Prefer graphics to text-only slides. Avoid cognitive overload: the audience should not be reading while you talk. Rules 1–5 are about the scope of each slide, 6–8 about its elements, 9–10 about the set of slides as a whole.

## The talk as a whole (Bourne 2007; Peyton Jones)
- Talk to the audience you have: their background and what they hope to get.
- Less is more: inexperienced speakers try to say too much. Make the points few and clear.
- Peyton Jones (paraphrased): the aim of a talk is to make people want to read the paper, not to replace it; motivate in the first minutes with a concrete example; present one key idea well; use examples; leave the full technical detail to the paper.
- Review a recording of your own talk; the problems become obvious.

## How the skill turns this into slides
- Slide titles come from the claims in `claims.md`, one claim per slide.
- The body is the evidence row of that claim: the figure, table or result, redrawn for a slide.
- Limitations get their own slide; the last main slide states the take-home message.
- Backup slides hold the answers to questions you expect.
