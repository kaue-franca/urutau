## A. Scientific method and philosophy of science (S01–S05)
_Reader: group A1._

### S01 · Scientific Method (SEP; Hepburn & Andersen, rev. 2026)
- Source used: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/ · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole main text of the revision dated 8 Aug 2026 (sections 1–7, including the new section 5.2 on computers and AI), but not the bibliography. Authors confirmed from the page metadata (Hepburn & Andersen).
- Core claim: No single fixed recipe is common to all of science and found only in science. The current view is a moderate pluralism that pays attention to practice. Methods are context-specific problem-solving procedures that are fallible, and analysing them is descriptive, critical and advisory at once. According to Hoyningen-Huene and Haack, what sets science apart is not unique methods but more careful and systematic use of ordinary ones. That means more care in ruling out alternative explanations, finding sources of error, linking results to existing knowledge, and opening claims to criticism. The entry also covers: hypothetico-deductive (H-D) testing and its limits; Popper; the Kuhn/Feyerabend challenge; the Fisher vs Neyman–Pearson and Bayesian vs frequentist/error-statistical debates; exploratory and data-driven research as legitimate; verification vs validation and the opacity of computer and AI methods; and published papers as tidied-up reconstructions of a messier process.
- Implications for the method:
  - Do not encode a fixed 5-step "scientific method" (the Dewey/textbook legend). Define a small set of **modes** (exploratory, confirmatory/testing, model building, reproduction) and require every unit of agent work to declare its mode. The entry treats exploratory, data-driven work as legitimate in its own right. Its findings should be recorded as *candidate hypotheses or regularities*, not as tested conclusions.
  - Keep a **chronological lab log** (prompts, agent actions, dead ends, changes of plan) separate from the **final report**. The entry cites Medawar and others: papers are retrospective reconstructions that hide the real order of work. The log keeps the forking paths that the report will tidy away.
  - Separate **verification** (does the agent's code compute what was intended: unit tests, assertions, checks against a hand-computed reference) from **validation** (do inferences about the real phenomenon hold: hold-out or external data, sanity checks against domain knowledge). The entry warns that "validated" can sound like a deductive guarantee and lead to overconfidence. Because agent/AI steps are opaque, they should leave artifacts a person can inspect.
  - Before a test, record the **inferential stance**: which framework (frequentist, Bayesian, estimation), the error tolerances, and the *consequences* of false positives vs false negatives. Neyman–Pearson and Rudner/Churchman argue that choosing a threshold involves value judgments that depend on the cost of error.
  - Add a **systematicity checklist** at sign-off (Hoyningen-Huene): Were alternative explanations excluded? Were error sources sought out? Is the claim connected to prior knowledge? Has it been exposed to critical review?
- Caveats: This is a survey, not a prescription, and its own conclusion is that method varies with context, so any fixed workflow is a local choice rather than "the" method. The AI material (5.2) is short and cites a young, unsettled literature (e.g. whether AI can provide novel evidence, or AI as a "collaborator"). It reports the claim that ML makes method "obsolete" together with rebuttals (e.g. that induction is never theory-neutral), and settles neither. The entry says reproducibility is a desired *outcome* of method rather than strictly part of it, which is a contestable framing. Interview studies it cites suggest scientists themselves struggle to turn general method into concrete strategy.

### S02 · Karl Popper (SEP; Thornton, rev. 2026)
- Source used: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/ · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole main text (substantive revision 31 Jul 2026), sections 1–12 including the Critical Evaluation, but not the bibliography. Author confirmed from the page metadata.
- Core claim: Universal claims cannot be verified by experience, but a genuine counter-instance can refute them. Science therefore advances by bold conjectures that are exposed to severe tests and to error elimination, following the schema problem → tentative theory → error elimination → new problem. Corroboration counts only when a prediction was risky, meaning it could have failed. The criteria for refutation must be fixed *beforehand*, and ad hoc rescues that serve only to avoid refutation are illegitimate. Observations are theory-laden, so tests end at "basic statements" that the community *decides* to accept. That acceptance is provisional and revisable, like a jury verdict ("piles driven into a swamp"). Criticism is piecemeal: some background knowledge is held fixed for the moment and can be challenged when trouble appears.
- Implications for the method:
  - **Refutation criteria before results**: for every confirmatory analysis, a committed file states the hypothesis, the specific prediction (direction, rough size, which subgroup), and *which outcome would count against it*. It is written before the agent touches the outcome data or test split. If no outcome could count against it, the hypothesis is labelled non-testable.
  - **Prefer high-content predictions**: "feature X raises conversion by 2–5 points in segment S, not in T" is better than "X is related to conversion". Record afterwards whether the prediction was risky, and give little weight to confirmations of predictions that could not have failed.
  - **Ad hoc modification log**: any change made after seeing results (new filter, dropped outliers, redefined metric, added covariate, new subgroup) is recorded with its reason. It must either bring a *new, independently testable* consequence or be marked as an ad hoc rescue. Popper's later, softer position allows modifications only if they are not purely face-saving.
  - **Accepted-facts and background-assumptions register**: list what is being treated as given, e.g. "labels are correct", "the join keys are unique", "the logging was not changed in March". When results conflict, revisit these entries one at a time (piecemeal criticism) instead of only blaming the hypothesis.
  - **Iteration ends with a new problem (P2)**: each cycle's write-up names the next problem the error-elimination step revealed. Do not discard a model or hypothesis until a better replacement exists. Also separate observed *trends* from *laws*: forecasts are conditional and depend on stated stability assumptions, since Popper argues unconditional prediction needs isolated, stationary, recurrent systems.
- Caveats: The entry's own critical section matters here. (1) Duhem–Quine/Putnam: a failed prediction always involves auxiliary hypotheses, so it cannot by itself show which part failed. (2) Lakatos: there are no clean crucial experiments; theories live with anomalies inside research programmes. (3) Accepting basic statements by convention makes the scheme partly conventionalist, which sits uneasily with Popper's realism. (4) Miller and Tichý showed that Popper's formal definitions of verisimilitude fail for false theories. (5) Popper finally conceded that falsifiability alone cannot demarcate science. Popper also rejects induction outright, which is in tension with the probabilistic and statistical inference that data science runs on (see S01 §4). Falsificationism is therefore a discipline for framing and testing claims, not a full account of evidence.

### S03 · Thomas Kuhn (SEP; Bird, rev. 2025)
- Source used: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thomas-kuhn/ · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole main text (substantive revision 2 Sep 2025), sections 1–7.5 including the posthumous *Plurality of Worlds* material, but not the bibliography. Author confirmed from the page metadata.
- Core claim: Most science is "normal science": puzzle-solving inside a disciplinary matrix of shared theories, instruments, values and especially *exemplars* (model solutions). Judging whether a solution is good works by perceived similarity to those exemplars, not by applying explicit rules. Anomalies are usually set aside or explained away. Only when troublesome anomalies pile up does a crisis lead to a revision of the matrix, a revolution, which can lose some earlier explanatory gains ("Kuhn-loss"). Theory choice is guided by shared values (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness), but these are imprecise and can be weighted differently, so reasonable people disagree. Observation, standards and vocabulary/taxonomy change with the framework, which makes cross-framework comparison hard (incommensurability).
- Implications for the method:
  - **Make the project's "disciplinary matrix" explicit**: a short framing document listing the accepted problem definition, metric definitions, standard methods and baselines, and the **exemplars**, i.e. reference analyses or notebooks that set the bar. If Kuhn is right that judgment runs on similarity to exemplars, then agent instructions should include worked example solutions, not just rules (my inference from the entry, not a claim it makes).
  - **Anomaly ledger**: every result that does not fit (unexpected sign, a segment that behaves differently, a metric that disagrees with another) is logged rather than quietly explained away. Set a rule (a count, or a periodic review) for when accumulated anomalies force a review of the framing itself rather than more patching.
  - **Model/hypothesis choice rubric** based on Kuhn's five values. Record the scores *and* the weights used, and why. Kuhn says the values cannot settle the choice on their own, so the weighting is a recorded judgment and not a computed verdict.
  - **Versioned definitions / data dictionary**: terms such as "active user", "churn" or "fraud" are taxonomies that shape what is observed. When a definition changes, mark results before and after as not directly comparable, and note what the old analysis explained that the new one does not (Kuhn-loss).
  - Use **diverse evaluators** (human review, a second agent with a different prompt or model) and do not force premature consensus. Kuhn sees disagreement within shared values as a way to spread the risk of novelty.
- Caveats: Kuhn is descriptive and works at the scale of whole scientific communities and historical eras. Mapping "paradigm/crisis/revolution" onto one practitioner's project is an analogy, not something the source licenses. The entry reports strong criticism: "paradigm" was used ambiguously (Masterman); Toulmin argued revisions are more frequent and gradual than Kuhn says; discoveries like DNA's structure do not fit the anomaly-driven model; realists rebut incommensurability with referential continuity. Kuhn's anti-realism about truth is a minority position. His defence of normal-science conservatism (ignoring anomalies) is in direct tension with Popper (S02). A method has to decide when holding the framework fixed is productive and when it is a blind spot.

### S04 · Understanding Science flowchart, text description (UC Museum of Paleontology, current page ©2026)
- Source used: https://undsci.berkeley.edu/the-understanding-science-flowchart-text-description/ · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole text description, which is short. I did not view the interactive or graphic flowchart itself, only its text rendering.
- Core claim: Real inquiry is non-linear and iterative rather than a linear recipe. It has many entry points (curiosity, a practical problem, new technology, serendipity, a surprising observation, personal motivation) and moves freely between four interconnected areas: exploration & discovery; testing ideas (hypothesis → expected results → actual results, then interpretation); community analysis & feedback (peer review, replication, discussion, publication, theory building); and benefits & outcomes. When data are interpreted they may support or oppose a hypothesis, inspire a revised or new hypothesis, or inspire *revised assumptions*.
- Implications for the method:
  - Treat the workflow as a **state graph, not a pipeline**. Each task records its entry point (e.g. "surprising observation in dashboard", "stakeholder problem") and its current area, and moving back (testing → exploration) is allowed and logged.
  - In testing, always write **expected results next to actual results** as two separate fields, with expected filled in first.
  - Make the interpretation step a **forced choice among four outcomes**: support, oppose, revise hypothesis, revise assumptions. The fourth option prompts a check of data, pipeline and framing assumptions, not only the hypothesis.
  - Build in a **community/feedback stage** even for solo work: an independent re-run (a fresh agent session or a person reproducing from the repo), a review of the write-up, and a short "publication" artifact.
  - Tie each project to its **outcome** (decision, product change, knowledge), so the feedback loop includes whether the result was useful.
- Caveats: This is an educational, simplified model meant to correct the textbook linear method. It gives no guidance on statistics, evidence weighting, or how to decide between outcomes. "Community" assumes a research community, and for a solo practitioner using agents it has to be simulated, which is weaker than independent peer review. I read the text description, not the visual, so any detail present only in the graphic is not covered.

### S05 · The Method of Multiple Working Hypotheses (T. C. Chamberlin, 1897; reprinted J. Geology 1995 with intro by D. Raup)
- Source used: https://www.eps.mcgill.ca/~courses/c590/Chamberlain%201897.pdf · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. It is a 7-page JSTOR scan of *J. Geology* 103(3):349–354 (1995), which reprints the revised 1897 version. I read Raup's one-page introduction and the whole essay. The original 1890 *Science* version was not read.
- Core claim: Chamberlin describes three mental methods. The "ruling theory" lets a premature explanation grow, through affection for one's own idea, from tentative to adopted to ruling. The mind then unconsciously selects and magnifies agreeing facts, neglects or presses the refractory ones, and mistakes repeated application of the idea to similar cases for added evidence. The single "working hypothesis" is better, because it is a means of finding facts, but it slides easily into a ruling theory. The remedy is to bring *every rational explanation* into view at the start and develop all of them impartially as a "family" of hypotheses. This divides the investigator's affections. It also allows for phenomena caused by several factors acting together, where the task is to estimate each factor's share. It sharpens discriminating criteria, and each hypothesis suggests its own tests. Order matters: first establish what the phenomenon actually is, then interpret it.
- Implications for the method:
  - **Mandatory rival-hypotheses step**: before any explanatory analysis, the agent lists all plausible explanations for the observed pattern. This must include the mundane ones: data artifact, pipeline bug, leakage, selection or survivorship effect, instrumentation or logging change, seasonality, chance. These go into a hypothesis register that is kept for the whole project.
  - **Discriminating-evidence matrix**: a table of hypotheses × planned checks showing what each hypothesis predicts. Prioritise checks where the rivals disagree, and do not run checks that every rival predicts equally.
  - **Expect several causes**: do not stop at the first factor that works. Where possible estimate the relative contribution of each factor (decomposition, ablation, attribution), and say explicitly when the explanation is only partial.
  - **Guard against the "repetition illusion"**: re-applying the same explanation to more cases of the same kind is not new evidence, and the report should not count it as such.
  - **Describe before explaining**: profiling and data validation (what is actually there, its ranges and coverage) come before causal storytelling. My own inference, not in the source: this is especially relevant to LLM agents, which readily produce one fluent explanation, so the method should ask for rivals rather than accept the first narrative.
- Caveats: Raup's introduction notes the essay is still controversial. Critics (Johnson 1990, "a chimera") argue that true impartiality is psychologically impossible and that productive science often runs on committed bias ("no progress without prejudice"). Listing *every* rational hypothesis has no natural limit, so a working method needs a way to prioritise. Chamberlin himself concedes that practical plans may follow one hypothesis at a time. His examples are geological, with complex, multi-cause phenomena, and some of his claims (e.g. general scepticism of simple explanations) may not carry over to domains where single causes are common. The essay gives no procedure for formal testing. Platt's later "Strong Inference" makes it operational.

### S06 · Strong Inference (Platt, 1964)
- Source used: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/science64_strong_inference.pdf (the given whoi.edu URL failed TLS verification through the session proxy, so I used this course-page copy) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole article and its notes. The copy is a transcription from a scratched microfilm and carries a warning that punctuation may differ from the original. None of the content points used here depends on punctuation.
- Core claim: Fields that move fastest (1960s molecular biology, high-energy physics) apply an old inductive routine every day, formally and explicitly. The routine: (1) write down alternative hypotheses, (2) design a crucial experiment whose possible outcomes each exclude one or more of them, (3) get a clean result, (4) recycle with sub-hypotheses, which builds a "logical tree". Platt takes Chamberlin's "multiple working hypotheses" from 1897 to stop people getting attached to one idea and to make disagreements between ideas rather than between people. He argues that only exclusions are secure. Measurement, surveys, tooling and quantitative fits without an exclusion are often busywork ("method-oriented" rather than "problem-oriented"). He offers "The Question" as a test: what experiment could disprove this hypothesis, or what hypothesis does this experiment disprove?
- Implications for the method:
  - Before any analysis or modelling run, the agent writes down at least two competing hypotheses (including a "boring" or null explanation such as an artefact, leakage or a sampling effect). It also records what each one predicts for the planned check.
  - Each experiment record states which hypothesis it is designed to exclude and what result would count as exclusion, before running it. If no hypothesis would be excluded by any result, that is a red flag ("busywork"): question the run or justify it as exploration.
  - Keep a persistent, append-only "logical tree" or lab notebook per project (Platt cites Fermi's and Faraday's notebooks): the question, the alternatives, the crucial checks, the outcomes and which branches were pruned. The agent updates it every session rather than reconstructing it at the end.
  - Make "The Question" a standing review prompt for every claim the agent produces ("what result would have shown this wrong?") and for every experiment it proposes ("what does this rule out?").
  - Prefer a coarse but decisive qualitative check (a "logical box") over a small gain in a quantitative fit metric. Treat agreement to a loose tolerance as weak evidence when several hypotheses predict it equally.
- Caveats: This is an essay arguing from selected success stories (survivorship), not empirical evidence that the method speeds progress. Later critics (for example O'Donohue & Buchanan 2001; Fudge 2014 — UNVERIFIED here, not read) argue that crucial experiments are rarely decisive (Duhem–Quine: auxiliary assumptions can always absorb a refutation) and that the field comparisons are anecdotal. Platt's own footnote concedes that Kuhn complicates the picture. It fits mechanistic, experimentally controllable questions best. Much data-science work is observational, predictive or descriptive, where clean exclusions are rare and "explore the data" is legitimate. His dismissal of quantitative and mathematical work is overstated, and Box (S08) and Breiman (S09) give the counterweight. Also, generating many hypotheses cheaply is easy for an LLM agent. The bottleneck is designing checks that actually discriminate between them.

### S07 · Cargo Cult Science (Feynman, 1974)
- Source used: https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/51/2/CargoCult.htm · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole commencement address in the Engineering & Science HTML transcription.
- Core claim: Much research copies the outward forms of science without the essential ingredient, so "the planes don't land". That ingredient is a specific scientific integrity: leaning over backwards to show how you might be wrong. In practice: report everything that could invalidate a result, the alternative explanations you ruled out and how, and the data that disagree as well as those that agree. Make sure a theory predicts something beyond the facts that suggested it. Decide to publish whichever way the result comes out. The first principle is not to fool yourself, because you are the easiest person to fool. His examples: the slow upward drift of the electron-charge measurements after Millikan (people looked harder for errors when results were far from the accepted value), refusing to replicate the baseline condition before changing it, and ignoring Young's rat-maze controls.
- Implications for the method:
  - Results reports carry a required "threats to validity / what could make this wrong" section. It lists alternative explanations, which ones were eliminated and by what check (with a link to that check), plus known contrary evidence. An agent's summary that has only supporting evidence is incomplete by rule.
  - Pre-commit to reporting: register the planned check and record its outcome whether it is positive, null or negative, so nothing is selectively surfaced. Keep a log of all runs, not just the winning ones.
  - Reproduce the baseline before changing anything: the agent first reproduces the existing or reference result (prior model, published number, previous pipeline output) in the current environment, then varies one condition.
  - Debug symmetrically. Apply the same scrutiny (bug hunts, re-runs) to results that look good or match expectations as to results that look bad. Record when debugging was triggered and why, to catch "Millikan drift".
  - Separate "data that generated the idea" from "data that tests it": a hypothesis the agent formed while looking at a dataset must be confirmed on something else (a holdout, a new sample, an independent prediction).
- Caveats: It is a speech, not a methodology. It gives principles and anecdotes but no procedures, and it says outright that the needed integrity is hard to put into words. The Millikan and Young stories are retold from memory; their historical accuracy is not checked here (UNVERIFIED). Its tone towards the social sciences is dismissive. It puts the burden on individual virtue. For agents, the useful translation is structural: the workflow must force the disclosures, because neither an agent nor a tired human will reliably volunteer them. "Report everything" also needs a summary layer, or reviewers drown in it.

### S08 · Science and Statistics (Box, 1976)
- Source used: https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1976-box.pdf (JSTOR scan of JASA 71(356):791–799, hosted on Gwern.net) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read all nine pages. The two-column text extraction is interleaved in places, but the full argument, sections and figures were readable.
- Core claim: Scientific learning is a motivated iteration between theory and practice: deduce consequences from a tentative hypothesis, compare them with data, and let the discrepancy (the "error signal" in a feedback loop) induce a revised hypothesis. Progress needs (i) flexibility and courage to seek out errors, especially your own, and not to fall in love with your model; (ii) parsimony, because "all models are wrong" and over-elaboration is a mark of mediocrity; (iii) worrying selectively about what is importantly wrong ("mice vs tigers"); and (iv) mathematics used as a servant of the problem. Using Fisher at Rothamsted, Box shows good methods emerging from close contact with real problems: residual analysis, randomization, design, and the analyst switching roles from "sponsor" of the model during estimation to "critic" during diagnostic checking. He warns against an "open loop" that degenerates into "cookbookery" (forcing every problem into a routine technique) or "mathematistry" (theory for its own sake that redefines the problem).
- Implications for the method:
  - Structure work as explicit iterations, each with: the current model or hypothesis, the deduced expectation, the observed discrepancy, and the revision it motivated. The discrepancy (residuals, failed predictions, surprising slices) is the main artefact to record, not just the headline metric.
  - Give the agent two phases with different instructions: a "sponsor" phase (fit and estimate as well as possible under the model) followed by a mandatory "critic" phase (residual analysis, diagnostic checks, deliberate search for misfit). Do not skip the critic phase when results look good.
  - Keep a "tigers list": rank known model inadequacies by their consequence for the decision or question. Spend effort on the top items, and record which known flaws are deliberately accepted as "mice".
  - Parsimony check: when the agent proposes adding complexity (features, parameters, pipeline stages), it must show the added complexity fixes a named discrepancy. Otherwise prefer the simpler model.
  - Anti-cookbook rule: before applying a default technique, the agent states the question's real objective and whether the method's assumptions (independence, distribution, design such as split-plot or blocking) match how the data were actually collected. Randomization and design information are recorded as data provenance.
- Caveats: This is a commemorative lecture built on a hagiographic Fisher narrative, not a methods paper. Procedures are illustrated rather than specified. "All models are wrong" can be misused as licence to never test. Box's own position is that you must still check which ways a model is wrong. The design-of-experiments emphasis assumes you can collect new data, which is often not true in observational data-science settings. It predates modern computation, so cross-validation, ML models and large-scale automation are not addressed. The small simulation of t-test vs Mann-Whitney under autocorrelation is illustrative only.

### S09 · Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures (Breiman, 2001, with comments by Cox, Efron, Hoadley, Parzen and rejoinder)
- Source used: https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~ryantibs/journalclub/breiman_2001.pdf (full Statistical Science 16(3):199–231 reprint with all four discussions and the rejoinder; the given projecteuclid.org URL returned a bot-block page) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the main paper, the Cox, Efron, Hoadley and Parzen comments, and Breiman's rejoinder.
- Core claim: Breiman contrasts a "data modeling" culture (assume a stochastic model for how the data were generated, estimate parameters, validate with goodness-of-fit and residuals) with an "algorithmic modeling" culture (treat the mechanism as unknown, find a function f(x), validate by predictive accuracy on held-out data). He argues that conclusions from data models are about the model, not nature. Omnibus goodness-of-fit tests and residual plots have little power in more than a few dimensions. Many quite different models fit equally well (the "Rashomon" effect), so variable-importance stories are unstable. Accuracy and interpretability conflict (the "Occam" dilemma). High dimensionality can help. Accurate black boxes, for example random forests with permutation importance, can yield more reliable information than weak interpretable models. In the discussion: Cox defends starting from a subject-matter question, probabilistic models for bias, measurement error and missingness, prediction under changed conditions, and shunning automatic model selection. Efron warns that new methods always look better in simulations (unequal tuning effort), that complex methods are harder to criticize, and that prediction alone is rarely the goal. Hoadley stresses test-set overfitting through "peeking", drift over time, interpretable but accurate scorecards, and optimizing business value instead of error. Parzen calls it the "error of the third kind" (right answer, wrong question). In the rejoinder Breiman concedes data models are useful when the mechanism is well understood or data are scarce, and that models must be refreshed when the context changes. He maintains that weak predictive accuracy undermines any claimed insight.
- Implications for the method:
  - Required predictive sanity check: any model used to draw substantive conclusions (coefficients, "important variables", effects) must report held-out or cross-validated accuracy against a trivial baseline (for example majority class or mean). If it barely beats the baseline, conclusions from it are flagged as unreliable (Breiman's hepatitis example: logistic regression 17.4% error vs 20.6% baseline).
  - Rashomon / stability check before stating "X is important": re-fit on bootstrap or perturbed samples and with alternative model families, and report how stable the variable ranking or story is. Check correlated surrogates (variables 12 and 17 in the hepatitis example) and report importance for groups, not just single variables.
  - Test-set hygiene: lock a final holdout that the agent cannot see during iteration, log every evaluation against it, and count "peeks". Give the baseline as much tuning budget as the challenger (Efron's Rule 1), and record the tuning budget per method.
  - Write a question card at the start that says whether the goal is prediction, explanation or causal effect, under which deployment conditions, and with what loss that reflects real costs (Hoadley: dollars saved at a fixed alert rate, not misclassification rate). Model choice and validation follow from the card, not from habit. This also protects against Parzen's "right answer to the wrong question".
  - For predictions under conditions unlike the training data (Cox), or deployment over time (Hoadley), require an explicit statement of the shift assumption, a temporal or out-of-distribution validation where possible, and a plan to monitor and recalibrate.
- Caveats: Cox calls the portrayal of "mainstream" statistics partly a caricature, and Breiman's "98% vs 2%" figures are rhetorical. Predictive accuracy does not validate causal or mechanistic claims: a model can predict well through confounded or leaky features, and Breiman's claim that more accurate means more reliable information is a heuristic, not a theorem. Permutation importance itself misbehaves with correlated features. Hoadley's "Ping-Pong theorem" shows importance depends on the feature set chosen. Efron and Hoadley both show cross-validation and test sets can be biased by tinkering and do not guarantee future performance. Interpretability has since become a much richer topic than "accuracy first, then explain" (later literature, not read here). The 2001 empirical comparisons are now dated.

### S10 · Philosophy and the practice of Bayesian statistics (Gelman & Shalizi, 2013)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.3868 (PDF of arXiv v4, 28 Jun 2011, "revised in response to referees"; the 2013 journal version in British Journal of Mathematical and Statistical Psychology 66:8–38 and its published discussion were not read) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full for the arXiv v4 text (all sections, footnotes and figure captions). The journal version's discussion and rejoinder were not accessed, and the arXiv text may differ slightly from the final published version (UNVERIFIED).
- Core claim: The "usual story" says Bayesian inference is inductive and science advances by updating posterior probabilities of competing models. The authors reject it: the best Bayesian practice is hypothetico-deductive, close to Mayo's error-statistics. Models (prior included) are deliberately approximate tools. You fit them, deduce their implications, and check them against data, mainly with posterior predictive checks and graphs, ideally severe tests aimed at errors that would undermine the specific inferences you care about. When a check fails, you expand or replace the model. Under misspecification, which is always the case, Bayesian updating only concentrates on the best approximation within the prior's support and cannot discover that the model class is wrong. So model checking happens outside the Bayesian calculus. They prefer continuous model expansion over choosing or averaging among discrete models by posterior probabilities or Bayes factors. They offer Kuhn's normal versus revolutionary science as a loose analogy for working within a model versus checking and replacing it. Worked examples: income and voting by state, where a varying-slopes model was found through graphical misfit, and redistricting effects, where rejecting the parallel-lines model changed the substantive conclusion.
- Implications for the method:
  - Every fitted model gets a required "can it reproduce the data?" step: simulate replicated data from the fitted model, or use held-out data, and compare it graphically with the real data on statistics chosen to detect the errors that matter for the question. Save the plots as artefacts linked to the model version.
  - Treat assumptions (priors, functional form, independence, default specifications such as "parallel lines" in before–after data) as testable hypotheses. Write them down explicitly in a model card and check the consequential ones. Record "the model is false, but is it false in a way that matters for this inference?"
  - When a check fails, record the direction of the misfit and the model expansion it motivated (for example adding an interaction or varying slopes). Prefer expanding to a model that contains the alternatives over choosing between them on fit statistics alone.
  - Design checks for severity: for each claim, ask "would this check have failed if the claim were wrong?" A passed check with low power counts as weak evidence and is labelled that way.
  - Revisit framing on several time scales: schedule periodic reassessments where the agent and human ask whether the problem formulation itself is wrong, not just the model. The authors note that such reframings happen within hours in consulting and within months in research.
- Caveats: The authors are statisticians writing on philosophy and acknowledge partial coverage. Philosophers and subjective Bayesians contest the anti-inductivist reading (the published discussion in BJMSP, not read here, includes such responses — UNVERIFIED). Posterior predictive checks have known power problems (they use the data twice; the authors cite Bayarri & Berger) and are conservative. Graphical checking is judgement-heavy and hard to automate or audit, which matters for agents because an agent can rationalize any plot. Repeated model expansion driven by the same data is itself a "garden of forking paths" risk, which the paper does not tackle. The examples come from social-science survey and observational data. Prediction-focused and ML settings are deliberately out of scope (footnote 21).

### S11 · Why Most Published Research Findings Are False (Ioannidis, 2005)
- Source used: https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.0020124 · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole article text (model, bias and multiple-team extensions, Box 1, six corollaries, the Table 4 discussion and the improvement section). Tables and figures were images, so I read only their captions and the text that discusses them. I did not read the linked companion critiques.
- Core claim: The probability that a claimed "significant" finding is true (the positive predictive value, PPV) depends on the pre-study odds R that the tested relationship is real, on statistical power and alpha, on bias u (design, analysis and reporting choices that turn non-findings into findings), and on how many teams test the same question. Under realistic values, PPV is often below 50%, especially for small studies, small effects, many tested relationships with little preselection, and flexible designs, definitions, outcomes and analyses. Conflicts of interest and "hot" fields lower it further. In fields where nothing real is being found, the reported effect sizes may simply measure the field's net bias. Very large or very significant effects should therefore raise suspicion rather than excitement.
- Implications for the method:
  - Before each confirmatory analysis, record an explicit prior-plausibility note: how likely the hypothesis is, how many candidate relationships or features are being screened, and the power or precision expected. Interpret any result against that note, not against p alone.
  - Count and log every relationship, model or feature set tested in the project, so the effective multiplicity is known (this is Ioannidis' "number and lesser preselection" factor). An agent can try hundreds of variants cheaply, so this count has to be automatic.
  - Add a "too good to be true" check. An unexpectedly large effect or near-perfect metric triggers a mandatory audit for leakage, duplicated rows, target-proxy features and evaluation bugs before anything is reported.
  - Prefer standardized, pre-agreed analysis templates and fixed primary outcomes and metrics for recurring analysis types. This reduces flexibility, which is his corollary 4.
  - Treat a single significant result as provisional until it is replicated on new data or a held-out split. Judge the totality of evidence, not one run.
- Caveats: The paper is a model-based argument. Its PPV numbers depend on assumed R, power and u, which are rarely known, and the title's "most" holds only under those assumptions. It treats findings as binary (p<0.05 = claimed). PLOS lists companion critiques next to the article, including a piece titled "Problems in the Analysis" (by Goodman and Greenland, per the page listing), but I did not read them. Later empirical estimates of false-discovery rates in some literatures came out lower than the paper's dramatic scenarios (UNVERIFIED, from memory, e.g. Jager & Leek 2014). The paper is framed around biomedical and epidemiological research. Applying it to predictive-modelling work requires treating "claimed relationship" as "claimed model improvement or feature effect."

### S12 · False-Positive Psychology (Simmons, Nelson & Simonsohn, 2011)
- Source used: SSRN (403) and SAGE (403) were blocked, and the Wayback copy failed (connection reset). I read the published version (Psychological Science 22:1359–1366, version of record) from a course-hosted copy: https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~schaller/528Readings/SimmonsNelsonSimonsohn2011.pdf · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. All 8 article pages were read, including Table 1 (simulations), Figs 1–2, Table 2 (requirements and guidelines), Table 3 (the compliant re-report), the criticisms and the "nonsolutions" section.
- Core claim: Undisclosed "researcher degrees of freedom" can push the real false-positive rate far above the nominal 5%. These include choosing among dependent variables, optional stopping or adding observations, optional covariates, and dropping conditions. In 15,000 simulations each, combining four common choices gave roughly 61% false positives at p<.05. Two real experiments "showed" that a song made listeners chronologically younger. The cause is ambiguity plus a self-serving interpretation of that ambiguity, not malice. The proposed fix is disclosure: six author requirements and four reviewer guidelines. The authors explicitly reject alpha correction, switching to Bayesian methods, conceptual replications and mere data posting as substitutes.
- Implications for the method:
  - Fix and record the stopping rule (sample size, data window, number of tuning or search iterations) before looking at outcomes. If the agent peeks at interim results, log every peek.
  - Keep a complete inventory of variables, outcomes, conditions, segments and models that were available or tried. Reports list all of them (the "only"-style exhaustive list), including failed variants.
  - Robustness disclosure is mandatory. For any exclusion or filter, also report the result without it. For any covariate or control, also report the result without it. Show results under alternative reasonable analytic choices.
  - Reward imperfect but honest results in reviewing the agent's output: a reviewer prompt or check that is suspicious of "perfect" patterns from small data.
  - When a choice cannot be justified convincingly, require an exact re-run of the same pipeline on fresh data or a sealed holdout before the claim is accepted. A "conceptual" rerun with different choices does not count.
- Caveats: The simulations use simple t-test or ANCOVA setups with n≈20 per cell. The magnitudes are illustrative, not universal. The paper itself admits the remedy does not solve the file-drawer problem and does not help against deliberate deception. The "at least 20 observations per cell" rule is a crude heuristic, and the authors later distanced themselves from it (UNVERIFIED, from memory). Its dismissal of Bayesian methods applies to flexibility, not to Bayesian inference in general. Psychology experiments are not identical to observational or ML data science, though the mechanisms (stopping, subsetting, covariate choice) carry over directly.

### S13 · The Garden of Forking Paths (Gelman & Loken, 2013)
- Source used: https://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/research/unpublished/p_hacking.pdf (14 Nov 2013 draft) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read all sections, from the theoretical framework through the examples (arm circumference, ESP, menstrual cycle and voting, red/pink clothing), the discussion, "possible reactions", "the way forward" and "toward a positive message."
- Core claim: A multiple-comparisons problem exists even when a researcher runs only one analysis, provided that the analysis choices would have differed had the data differed. These choices include exclusion and coding rules, main effect versus interaction, subgroups, pooling, and which outcome to use. Such data-contingent choices feel natural and theory-driven, not like "fishing," so honest researchers underestimate them. A published p-value cannot then be taken at face value. The authors reject the idea that this implies misconduct. They argue that most work without preregistration should be treated as exploratory. Their remedies are preregistered replication where new data are cheap, and, in observational settings, analysing all relevant comparisons (e.g. multilevel models) rather than one highlighted comparison. They also endorse a sharper split between exploratory and confirmatory work.
- Implications for the method:
  - Make the "forking paths" explicit. Before analysis, the agent writes down the decision points it expects (exclusions, transformations, subgroups, metric definitions, feature sets) and how each will be resolved. During analysis, it logs every point where the data influenced a choice.
  - Label each result as confirmatory (plan fixed before outcome data were seen) or exploratory (data-contingent). Report p-values or "significance" language only for confirmatory results. Present exploratory results as estimates with uncertainty plus a statement of fragility.
  - Use a two-stage design: explore on one split, then freeze the pipeline (hash or commit) and confirm on an untouched split or new data. Gelman likens this to external validation and cross-validation practice.
  - When new data are impossible (public or observational datasets already seen), report all relevant comparisons, or a hierarchical model or multiverse over them, instead of the single best-looking one.
  - Check for a gap between the scientific hypothesis and the statistical test. If several different data patterns would all "confirm" the hypothesis, the hypothesis is too vague to be tested by the analysis.
- Caveats: This is an unpublished working paper (a version later appeared in American Scientist, 2014, per the ASA editorial's reference list). Its framing is frequentist critique, and it relies on counterfactual reasoning about what an analyst would have done. Gelman himself says full preregistration is often impractical for applied work on familiar public data, and that iterative data analysis is where the most valuable insights come from. So this source argues for awareness and transparency more than strict rules. The multilevel-modelling remedy is acknowledged as non-trivial and sensitive to assumptions when comparisons are few. The examples are all small-sample social psychology.

### S14 · ASA Statement on p-Values (Wasserstein & Lazar / ASA, 2016)
- Source used: The URL given (https://www.amstat.org/asa/files/pdfs/p-valuestatement.pdf) is only the 2-page ASA press release, and I read it fully. For the full statement I read The American Statistician editorial plus the statement, Wasserstein & Lazar 2016, 70(2):129–133 (doi 10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108; the tandfonline page was blocked with 403), from https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/Real_World/ASA_statement.pdf · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full (editorial, the six principles with explanations, "other approaches", conclusion). I did not read the ~20 online discussion supplements.
- Core claim: The statement gives a consensus definition: a p-value is the probability, under a specified model, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. It sets out six principles. (1) A p-value shows incompatibility of the data with a specified model. (2) It is not the probability that a hypothesis is true or that chance alone produced the data. (3) Conclusions and decisions should not rest only on a threshold. (4) Proper inference needs full reporting and transparency: disclose the number of hypotheses explored, all data-collection decisions, all analyses and all p-values computed. Selective reporting makes p-values uninterpretable even without formal multiple testing. (5) A p-value does not measure effect size or importance. (6) A p-value alone is weak evidence, and p≈0.05 in particular is only weak evidence. The statement also lists complementary approaches: estimation and intervals, Bayesian methods, likelihood ratios and Bayes factors, decision-theoretic modelling and FDR.
- Implications for the method:
  - Results are reported as effect sizes or metric deltas with uncertainty intervals and units, with the context of design, data quality and prior evidence. A p-value, if used, is never the sole decision criterion. A rule could be: "no claim stated only as 'significant'."
  - Track a per-project "analysis ledger" that counts all hypotheses explored and all tests or p-values computed. Reports state how many analyses were run and how the reported ones were selected (principle 4).
  - When an analysis informs a business or product decision, write the decision rule and the costs of errors explicitly rather than using p<0.05 as the gate.
  - Make the agent (and the human) state model assumptions behind each test. A small p-value is evidence against the whole model (assumptions included), not just the null.
  - Never write "no effect" from a large p-value. Use interval estimates or equivalence tests (see S15).
- Caveats: The statement deliberately avoids power, error types and alternative hypotheses, and the editorial records strong disagreement among contributors. It is a statement of principles, not a procedure. Later ASA-related documents went further: the 2019 TAS editorial recommended abandoning "statistically significant", and a 2021 ASA presidential task force statement defended appropriately used p-values. Both are UNVERIFIED, not read here. The statement is aimed at non-statisticians, so its guidance is necessarily general.

### S15 · Equivalence tests (Lakens, 2017) · Equivalence testing tutorial (Lakens, Scheel & Isager, 2018)
- Source used: (a) Lakens 2017, SPPS 8(4):355–362, full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5502906/ · depth: full. (b) Lakens, Scheel & Isager 2018: SAGE (403) and the TU/e repository PDF (403) were blocked. I read the "In Press, AMPPS" author manuscript from OSF, https://osf.io/download/5a0f36936c613b026c4a1848/ (PsyArXiv v3zkt; 28 pp.) · depth: full. The manuscript states the updated preprint is identical in content to the published version, which I could not confirm directly. Both accessed 2026-10-26.
- Core claim: A non-significant test cannot support "no effect." The standard misuse (concluding absence from p>α) is common. Equivalence testing (TOST) lets one statistically reject effects at least as large as a pre-specified smallest effect size of interest (SESOI). Equivalence is concluded when the 90% CI lies entirely inside the bounds. Combined with a standard test, there are four outcomes: equivalent, different, both (trivially small but non-zero), or undetermined. The 2018 tutorial ranks ways to justify the SESOI. Best are objective grounds such as theory, just-noticeable or minimal clinically important differences, and cost-benefit. Next come prior studies ("small telescopes", the smallest effect an original study could detect) and resource-based bounds. Benchmarks such as d=0.5 are the weakest justification. Bounds must be fixed before seeing data. Bounds chosen after seeing data make the test meaningless.
- Implications for the method:
  - Every hypothesis or experiment spec records a SESOI (raw units preferred when meaningful, e.g. "0.5 pp of conversion", "0.01 AUC"), with its justification category. This is written before outcome data are seen.
  - Default reporting for comparisons (A/B tests, model A vs model B, feature ablations) includes both the difference test and an equivalence or non-inferiority test against the SESOI. Results are classified into the four outcomes, and "undetermined" is reported as such.
  - A rule for the agent: never write "no difference" or "no effect" unless an equivalence test against the pre-specified bounds passed. Otherwise write "inconclusive at this sample size."
  - Use power or sample-size analysis for equivalence too, so "inconclusive" is anticipated rather than discovered. Use small meta-analyses across repeated runs or studies when single runs are underpowered.
  - Use one-sided non-inferiority tests when only one direction matters (e.g. "the new cheaper model is not worse by more than Δ"). This choice must also be fixed in advance.
- Caveats: Both papers are psychology-oriented tutorials built on Neyman–Pearson frequentist logic. Their value rests entirely on how well the SESOI is justified, and that judgment remains subjective. Equivalence holds only for the specific operationalization, and confounds or incompetent execution can produce spurious equivalence (Lakens 2017 notes this asymmetry). The papers mention alternatives (ROPE, Bayes factors, plain estimation) without settling which is best. Standardized bounds depend on the sample SD. Narrow bounds need very large samples (e.g. n≈1,700 per group for d=±0.1 at 80% power). The software references (TOSTER versions, spreadsheet) are dated.

### S16 · The Preregistration Revolution (Nosek, Ebersole, DeHaven & Mellor, 2018)
- Source used: PNAS (403) was blocked. I read the full text at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856500/ · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. This covered all sections, including the nine practical "challenges" and the norm-building section.
- Core claim: The paper separates prediction (testing ideas with new data) from postdiction (generating explanations from data already seen). Confusing the two, via hindsight bias, motivated reasoning and the forking paths, gives p-values of unknown diagnosticity and overconfident claims. Preregistration means committing the analysis plan before observing outcomes, in a time-stamped registry. Its purpose is not to ban exploration but to make clear which analyses are which. The paper gives practical workarounds for imperfect cases. Deviations are documented. Assumption checks are handled through staged preregistration, blinded or scrambled data, preregistered decision trees, or standard operating procedures (SOPs). Pre-existing data call for registering what was already known. Many-experiment labs can use templates or treat early runs as exploratory and replicate. Discovery work can use a sealed holdout split, and every preregistered outcome must be reported. The authors acknowledge that experimental evidence for preregistration's benefit was still lacking at the time.
- Implications for the method:
  - Use a lightweight preregistration file per question (hypothesis, data, primary metric, exclusions, model or test, decision rule, SESOI). Commit it to version control so the git timestamp precedes any outcome-revealing run. The agent must not modify it after the fact. Changes go into a separate, dated deviations log with a reason and a note on whether outcomes had been seen.
  - Holdout sealing: split data early. The confirmatory split is inaccessible to the exploring agent (separate path, permissions, or checksum check) until the pipeline is frozen.
  - For data-dependent but outcome-blind steps (outlier rules, distribution checks, transformations), let the agent work on outcome-masked or label-shuffled data, or follow a pre-written decision tree or SOP. Reusable SOPs suit recurring project types.
  - For pre-existing or public data, the prereg records "what was already seen, by whom, and what summaries were read." This includes what the agent read in earlier sessions or notes.
  - Report all preregistered outcomes, including nulls, and keep the narrative from focusing only on the hits (Challenge 9). Give a programme-level tally such as "1 of 20 experiments positive."
- Caveats: The authors were employed by the Center for Open Science, which runs OSF and the preregistration programmes they recommend, and they declare this conflict. The case for preregistration is mainly theoretical plus correlational, as they acknowledge. It is framed around NHST. Critics argue preregistration does not fix weak theory or vague hypotheses, and that compliance with registered plans is often poor. The second point is supported by evidence the paper itself cites (Franco et al.; COMPare). The first criticism is from memory and UNVERIFIED as to specific authors. Decision trees and SOPs can themselves encode bias, a risk the paper notes.

### S17 · A Manifesto for Reproducible Science (Munafò et al., 2017)
- Source used: The ORCA Cardiff page returned a Cloudflare challenge (403). I read the open-access published article at https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-016-0021 (Nature Human Behaviour 1:0021) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full for the text (problem statement, Methods, Reporting and dissemination, Reproducibility, Evaluation, Incentives, Boxes 1–6, conclusion). ### S18 · Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational Research (Sandve, Nekrutenko, Taylor & Hovig, 2013)
- Source used: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003285 (PLOS Comput Biol HTML) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full (introduction and all ten rules).
- Core claim: Computational reproducibility, the minimum standard where independent replication is infeasible, depends on habits more than tools. The ten rules are: track how every result was produced as an executable workflow; avoid manual data manipulation; archive exact versions of external programs; version-control all custom scripts; keep intermediate results in standard formats; record random seeds; store the raw data behind plots; produce hierarchical output that can be drilled into; connect textual statements to the underlying results; and make scripts, runs and results public. Each rule has a stated minimum fallback. Reproducibility also pays off for the researcher's future self.
- Implications for the method:
  - Every reported number, table or figure has a provenance record: code commit hash, command or entry point, parameters, input data version or checksum, environment lockfile, seed, and timestamp. Ideally a single make or pipeline target regenerates it.
  - No manual edits to data. Any agent or human transformation is a script in the repo, and unavoidable manual steps are logged with what was changed and why.
  - Pin environments (lockfiles or containers). Record seeds for every stochastic step, and note which steps are stochastic, so exact versus approximate reproduction can be told apart.
  - Persist intermediate artifacts and the data behind each plot, in standard formats, with a naming convention that links summaries to their underlying detail.
  - Each claim in notes or reports carries a pointer (path, run ID, or commit) to the exact result supporting it. A check can flag claims without a pointer. This is directly enforceable for agent-written summaries.
- Caveats: The tool examples are dated (GenePattern, Galaxy, Taverna, Sweave). The rules address computational reproducibility, getting the same numbers again, not replicability or validity. A perfectly reproducible pipeline can still be p-hacked or wrong. The rules are expert opinion, not evidence-tested. Rule 10 (public release) may conflict with private or proprietary data.

### S19 · Ten Simple Rules for a Computational Biologist's Laboratory Notebook (Schnell, 2015)
- Source used: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4565690/ · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full (introduction and all ten rules).
- Core claim: Dry-lab scientists also need a lab notebook, as an organizational tool, memory aid, primary research record and legal record. The notebook should be chronological and contemporaneous. - Implications for the method:

  - Never delete or silently rewrite past entries. Corrections are new entries that reference the old one, which preserves failed attempts and the history of beliefs (this also protects against hindsight bias, see S16).

Much of it concerns paper notebooks, legal or IP validity (signatures, bound pages) and institutional ownership in a US academic setting, which is mostly irrelevant to a personal workflow. ### S20 · Documenting Architecture Decisions (Nygard, 2011)
- Source used: https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full (the whole blog post, including the experience report).
- Core claim: Decisions are made incrementally, and their motivation is the hardest thing to recover later. Without it, newcomers either accept old decisions blindly or reverse them blindly. Nygard therefore proposes Architecture Decision Records (ADRs): short (1–2 page), sequentially numbered, never-reused text files kept in the repo. Each has a Title, a value-neutral Context (the forces in tension), the Decision ("We will…"), a Status (proposed, accepted, deprecated or superseded, with a link) and Consequences (all of them, not just the positive ones). Reversed decisions are kept and marked superseded. Each ADR is written as a conversation with a future developer.
- Implications for the method:
  - Use "analysis decision records" for significant methodological choices: target and metric definition, exclusion rules, train/test split strategy, model class, handling of leakage-prone features, SESOI and decision thresholds. Each gets Context, Decision, Status and Consequences, including negative ones.
  - Number them sequentially, keep them in the repo, and make them immutable once accepted. A change is a new record that supersedes the old one, which gives an audit trail of when (relative to seeing results) each choice was made. This complements the prereg and deviation logs in S16.
  - Agents should read the active decision records before starting work, and must not silently contradict an accepted decision. To change it, they propose a new record with status "proposed" for human acceptance.
  - Keep records small and modular, one decision each, because "large documents are never kept up to date." This is a useful rule for agent context files as well.
- Caveats: This is a practitioner blog post about software architecture, not a scientific or evidence-based source. The experience report covers about 6–10 developers over a few months. It does not address scientific inference, and it says nothing about recording when a decision was made relative to seeing data, which a scientific adaptation must add. Deciding what counts as "significant" is left to judgment.

Later steps refer back to it. In particular, 3g asks why the sampling frame represents the 1a population, and 8b asks where the finding is not expected to hold.
  - **Keep a filled REFORMS checklist as a living file in the repo.** Each item points to the file, notebook cell or commit that satisfies it, or gives a written reason for "N/A". The agent updates it at milestones, not only at the end. The paper says it is useful during conceptualization and implementation as well as for write-up.
  - **Reproducibility bundle (module 2) as a hard gate.** The bundle needs:
    - a dataset identifier or hash and version;
    - the code commit;
    - environment capture: hardware, OS, package versions or a lockfile, and wall-clock time;
    - a README;
    - one reproduction script that regenerates every reported number and figure.

    Where data cannot be shared, the paper suggests a synthetic stand-in.
  - **Data-provenance record (module 3).** It covers sources and when they were collected, how labels were produced, the sampling frame, the outcome variable's definition and how far it is a proxy for the intended construct, sample size (individuals as well as rows), and missingness by class.
  - **Preprocessing log (4a–4c).** Record every exclusion with its rationale, how impossible or corrupt values were handled, and every transformation from raw data, ideally as a flow chart. Imputation and over/undersampling must be fitted per fold.
  - **Modeling record (5a–5f).** Record:
    - every model trained, not only the reported one;
    - the split or CV design, with per-split sizes;
    - how the final model was selected;
    - the hyperparameter ranges and why they are reasonable;
    - evidence that baselines got the same tuning effort.

    Pair this with the leakage justifications (6a–6c) and uncertainty estimates with their method (7b). 7c says to prefer reporting uncertainty over relying only on significance tests.
- Caveats:
  - REFORMS is a *reporting* standard. Filling it in does not guarantee validity, and self-reported answers can give false assurance.
  - It deliberately takes no side in open methodological debates.
  - Its scope excludes ML-methods research, pure predictive analytics or engineering, explanatory statistical modeling and simulation. Much day-to-day data-science work is therefore only partly in scope.
  - The authors say it fits predictive modeling better than ML used as a search tool, and that it is less useful when a result can be cheaply verified (e.g. in a lab).
  - The template is still labelled "beta", and item wording differs slightly between arXiv Table 2 and the current template (e.g. 3d/3e). Pin the version you use.
  - Proprietary foundation models are named as a grey area because they are non-deterministic and change silently, which matters directly for LLM-agent pipelines.

### S22 · Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in ML-based science (Kapoor & Narayanan, Patterns 4(9):100804, 2023)
- Source used: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856 (full-text HTML) + the model info sheet template at https://reproducible.cs.princeton.edu/model-info-sheet-template.docx · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the main article and the full 21-question model info sheet template. I did not read the supplemental PDF (mmc1: robustness checks, details of the civil-war reanalysis).
- Core claim:
  - A survey of 22 prior reviews found leakage in all 17 fields covered, affecting at least 294 papers. The authors stress this is a lower bound, not a systematic meta-review.
  - They propose a taxonomy of 8 leakage types under 3 headings:
    - **L1, no clean train/test separation:** no test set; preprocessing on train+test; feature selection on train+test; duplicates.
    - **L2:** illegitimate features (proxies for the outcome, or features unavailable at prediction time).
    - **L3, test set not drawn from the distribution of scientific interest:** temporal leakage; non-independence between train and test; sampling bias.
  - In a reanalysis of civil-war prediction, every paper claiming complex ML beat logistic regression (4 of 12 with code) had leakage. After fixing it, the ML models were not substantively better.
  - 9 of the 12 papers reported no significance tests or uncertainty.
- Implications for the method:
  - **A leakage audit is a required artifact** for any predictive claim. Adapt the model info sheet's 21 questions, which ask for:
    - every preprocessing, modeling and evaluation step;
    - for each step, how the train/test split was respected;
    - how duplicates and dependencies (patient, user, family, time) were handled;
    - why the test set represents the claim population;
    - why the temporal ordering holds;
    - a legitimacy argument covering every feature.
  - **Feature legitimacy as a written argument.** For each feature or group of features, state when it becomes available relative to the prediction time and why it is not a proxy for the outcome. Agents that engineer features automatically are an obvious source of L2 leakage, so this justification should be checked by a human or a separate reviewer, not self-certified by the agent that built the features.
  - **Mechanical checks the agent can run:**
    - assert that fit/transform objects are fitted only inside the training fold (pipelines);
    - test for exact and near duplicates across splits;
    - check group overlap across splits for known IDs;
    - check temporal ordering, i.e. max(train time) < min(test time) when the claim is about forecasting.
  - **Always include a simple, decades-old baseline** (e.g. logistic or linear regression) and report uncertainty on the comparison, for example bootstrap CIs on the test set. The paper's reanalysis shows CIs that are large relative to the claimed gains.
  - The paper's 5 diagnoses and recommendations translate into project-level habits:
    - state the expected limits to prediction;
    - treat results as tentative;
    - involve domain expertise;
    - prefer standardized splits and metrics (the common task framework) where possible;
    - ensure computational reproducibility, since claims on an info sheet cannot be verified without the code.
- Caveats:
  - The definition of leakage is deliberately broad. It includes sampling bias and distribution mismatch, which others would call external-validity problems rather than leakage, so the term is contested.
  - The survey is a convenience sample of published critiques, not a prevalence estimate.
  - Model info sheets rely on honest, competent self-report. The authors acknowledge they cannot be verified without code and data and need ML expertise to fill in.
  - The case study is one field.

### S23 · How to avoid machine learning pitfalls: a guide for academic researchers (Lones; arXiv 2108.02497v5, 29 Aug 2024; v5 published as "Avoiding machine learning pitfalls", Patterns 2024)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.02497 (v5, confirmed as latest on the abs page) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the whole guide: change log, all sections and the reference list.
- Core claim: a dos-and-don'ts guide that the author updates every year. It covers five stages:
  - **before building:** 8 items;
  - **reliable building:** 8 items;
  - **robust evaluation:** 8 items;
  - **fair comparison:** 6 items;
  - **reporting:** 6 items.

  Its central concern is preventing test-set information from leaking into model configuration, training or selection, including informal "sequential overfitting" from re-using a test set across iterations. It also stresses honest, multi-perspective reporting.
- Implications for the method:
  - **Partition and lock the test set at project start.** Keep it out of exploratory analysis ("don't look at all your data") and use it once, on the final model. Use separate validation sets, possibly several, for iteration. This matters especially for agents, which iterate fast and can silently overfit a test set they re-evaluate on every loop. The method should make test-set access an explicit, logged, rare event.
  - **Treat the whole pipeline as training.** Scaling, imputation, feature selection, PCA or dimensionality reduction, augmentation and oversampling are fitted only on training folds. Use nested CV when tuning, and grouped or stratified CV where needed. For time series use blocked or forward-chaining CV to avoid look-ahead bias.
  - **Evaluation records:**
    - multiple seeds and/or resamples, with spread (SD or CI) reported;
    - metrics chosen for class imbalance, e.g. MCC or kappa rather than accuracy;
    - partial metrics such as precision/recall;
    - state exactly which F-score or AUC variant was used;
    - save per-sample predictions so paired tests such as McNemar's can be run later;
    - fairness checks across relevant groups where people are affected.
  - **Comparison discipline:**
    - re-implement and tune all compared models to the same budget;
    - include naive or dummy baselines (majority class, persistence forecast) and simple ones (trees, linear models), as well as SOTA;
    - use non-parametric tests when distributional assumptions fail;
    - correct for multiple comparisons;
    - report effect sizes, not only p-values;
    - distrust small gains on community benchmarks, especially with foundation models whose training data may include the test set.
  - **Reporting:**
    - use experiment tracking (MLflow is named) and share scripts;
    - report what did not work;
    - don't generalize beyond the datasets used;
    - inspect models with XAI or ablations to detect spurious correlations, while knowing the limits of XAI;
    - fill in a checklist such as REFORMS early, as a planning tool.
- Caveats:
  - This is an informal, opinionated guide from one author, aimed at newcomers and academic publishing. It is not empirical evidence, and some advice is framed as what helps get published.
  - Some statistical advice is simplified, e.g. the description of significance thresholds and the suggestion of KS as an effect size.
  - The author is a REFORMS co-author, so the two sources are not independent.
  - Content changes between versions, so cite the version (v5 here).

### S24 · Improving reproducibility in ML research: a report from the NeurIPS 2019 reproducibility program (Pineau, Vincent-Lamarre, Sinha, Larivière, Beygelzimer, d'Alché-Buc, Fox, Larochelle; JMLR 22(164), 2021)
- Source used: https://jmlr.org/papers/volume22/20-303/20-303.pdf (linked from https://jmlr.org/papers/v22/20-303.html) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read all 20 pages. The checklist in Fig. 8 is an image, so I rendered it and read it visually.
- Core claim: NeurIPS 2019 ran three measures together: a voluntary code-submission policy, a community reproducibility challenge (173 papers claimed for reproduction), and a mandatory ML Reproducibility Checklist, v1.2. The checklist has 17 items in 3 groups:
  - models and algorithms;
  - theoretical claims;
  - figures and tables with empirical results: data collection, a dataset link, exclusions and preprocessing, how data was allocated to train/val/test, hyperparameter range plus selection method plus final values, the exact number of evaluation runs, how experiments were run, the metric definition, error bars, central tendency and variation, and compute infrastructure.

  Code submission rose to about 75%. One third of reviewers found the checklist useful. The authors explicitly say they have no conclusive evidence that the program improved paper quality.
- Implications for the method:
  - **Adopt the four-way distinction from the paper's Fig. 1** and say which one a check addresses:
    - reproducible: same data and same code;
    - replicable: different data;
    - robust: same data, different analysis or implementation;
    - generalisable: different data and different analysis.

    A rerun by an agent only demonstrates the first.
  - **Per-result run record.** For every reported table or figure, record:
    - the exact number of runs and seeds;
    - the hyperparameter search space, how the search was done and the chosen values;
    - how data was allocated to train/val/test;
    - the metric definition;
    - the error-bar definition (SD, SE or CI, and over what);
    - the compute used.

    These are the checklist items authors most often claimed were "not applicable" (36% said error bars were N/A).
  - The paper lists sources of irreproducibility as a pre-mortem list for the agent to check: data mismatch; under-specified model or training; missing or buggy code; under-specified metrics; improper statistics; selective reporting or adaptive overfitting; over-claiming.
  - **Independent re-implementation has value beyond re-running the authors' code**, because shared code can replicate its own mistakes. A "fresh-context agent reproduces from the write-up" step is a cheap version of the reproducibility challenge.
  - **Containerize the environment.** The paper suggests Docker as a standard way to package code, data and OS.
- Caveats:
  - This is a descriptive program report. The observed associations between checklist answers, scores and acceptance are not causal, and the checklist's accuracy and benefit to authors are explicitly left open.
  - It targets ML-methods papers, not domain-science claims; REFORMS notes, for instance, that it does not ask about the target population.
  - Checklist answers were self-reported. Later NeurIPS checklists have superseded v1.2 (I did not check them here).

### S25 · Troubling trends in machine learning scholarship (Lipton & Steinhardt; arXiv 1807.03341v2, 26 Jul 2018; ICML 2018 Debates workshop)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.03341 (v2, latest) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the full essay; the reference list was skimmed.
- Core claim: ML papers increasingly show four patterns:
  1. presenting speculation as explanation;
  2. failing to identify where empirical gains come from (no ablations; gains that actually come from tuning);
  3. "mathiness", where math is used to impress rather than clarify, including spurious theorems;
  4. misused language: suggestive or anthropomorphic definitions, overloaded technical terms, and "suitcase words" such as interpretability or generalization.

  The authors speculate that the causes are complacency in the face of progress, rapid community growth that thins the reviewer pool, and misaligned incentives from media and investors. They recommend asking "what worked and why", not just "how well".
- Implications for the method:
  - **Label every statement in the write-up as one of three kinds:**
    - a claim backed by evidence, linked to a result;
    - a hypothesis or speculation, kept in a clearly marked section;
    - an open question.

    LLM agents readily write fluent causal explanations of results, so the method should require each explanatory sentence to cite an experiment or be marked speculative.
  - **Ablations and error analysis are required whenever a change has several parts.** Credit a gain to a component only after an ablation. Run a tuning-matched baseline before attributing a gain to architecture. Do robustness checks across hyperparameters and, ideally, datasets.
  - **A terminology check before reporting:**
    - avoid anthropomorphic or "human-level" phrasing;
    - define overloaded terms (generalization, bias, fairness, covariate shift) precisely;
    - state which sense of "generalize" is meant: train→test, transfer to another population, or external validity.
  - **Theory hygiene.** Include a formal statement only if it supports the claim. The essay's own test is whether you would rely on the explanation to make predictions or to get a system to work.
  - **Report negative results.** The essay suggests valuing a simple idea plus honest negative results over an unablated stack of tweaks. In practice: keep a log of failed attempts and report it.
- Caveats:
  - This is an explicitly non-balanced position paper. It contains no systematic measurement of whether the trends are actually rising.
  - Its examples are chosen illustrations, and the authors include their own work among them.
  - It concedes that the counter-arguments have merit: speed versus rigor (e.g. AlexNet shipped without ablations), and high standards possibly suppressing speculative ideas.
  - It concerns scholarship and writing, not statistical procedure.

### S26 · Deep reinforcement learning that matters (Henderson, Islam, Bachman, Pineau, Precup, Meger; AAAI 2018; arXiv 1709.06560v3, 30 Jan 2019)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.06560 (v3, latest) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the main text in full. The supplementary material (literature tables of hyperparameters and reported results, extra learning curves and significance tables) was skimmed only. pdftotext reported a recoverable xref warning, but the text extracted completely.
- Core claim: in policy-gradient continuous control (TRPO, PPO, DDPG, ACKTR on MuJoCo), reported performance depends heavily on several factors:
  - **extrinsic:** hyperparameters, network architecture, reward scale, and codebase or implementation, where different implementations of the "same" algorithm differ greatly;
  - **intrinsic:** random seeds and environment dynamics.

  Two groups of 5 seeds with identical settings produced statistically different learning curves. Reported results routinely use fewer than 5 trials or top-N selection. The authors recommend:
  - reporting all hyperparameters and implementation details;
  - matching baselines to the original results;
  - running many seeds;
  - reporting bootstrap CIs, significance tests and power analysis.
- Implications for the method:
  - **Seed policy fixed in advance.** Decide the number of seeds and the seed list before running. Run baselines and new methods on the same seeds. Never report best-of-N or top-N, and log every run, including failures. A bootstrap power analysis (simulate a lift and check detection rates) can set the number of seeds.
  - **Baseline sanity check.** A re-implemented or library baseline must reproduce the originally reported numbers under the original settings before it is used for comparison. Record which codebase and version supplies each baseline.
  - **Report the full configuration** for both baseline and new method: architecture, activations, reward scaling, normalization, codebase, environment version (e.g. Hopper-v1 vs later versions), and evaluation protocol.
  - **Look at behaviour, not just returns.** Videos or rollouts reveal degenerate local optima that still score well, such as Hopper surviving without hopping or Swimmer flailing. For an agent workflow, add a step that renders or inspects the learned behaviour.
  - **Evaluate on multiple environments** chosen in advance, not only the ones that look favourable. State whether evaluation follows the online view or the policy-optimization view.
- Caveats:
  - The scope is narrow: a few policy-gradient algorithms, 2–4 MuJoCo v1 tasks, 5 seeds in most experiments.
  - The recommended t-test and KS tests were not validated for RL assumptions, as the paper acknowledges.
  - Later work (S27) argues against dichotomous significance testing and shows bootstrap CIs over a single task with few runs are unreliable.
  - The environments and versions are now dated. The lessons are general, but the specific numbers are not.

### S27 · Deep RL at the edge of the statistical precipice (Agarwal, Schwarzer, Castro, Courville, Bellemare; NeurIPS 2021 Outstanding Paper; arXiv 2108.13264v4, 5 Jan 2022)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264 (v4, latest) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full main text (sections 1–6, including Table 1 recommendations and the case studies). I did not read the long appendices (A.1–A.7: bootstrap methods, protocol pitfalls, extra figures) in full.
- Core claim:
  - With the 3–10 runs typical of deep RL, point estimates of aggregate performance (mean or median normalized score) are dominated by statistical uncertainty.
  - In an Atari 100k case study with 100 runs per algorithm, reported medians were often far from the expected median. Narrow median CIs needed roughly 50–100 runs. Non-standard protocols, such as max-over-training scores, explained much of some claimed gains.
  - The paper recommends three tools for few-run reporting, released as the `rliable` library:
    - **stratified-bootstrap CIs** on aggregates;
    - **performance profiles** (run-score distributions with confidence bands);
    - **robust aggregates:** interquartile mean (IQM), optimality gap, and probability of improvement.

  Many published improvements on ALE, Procgen and DMC lie within overlapping CIs, or are only 50–70% likely to be real.
- Implications for the method:
  - **Treat every reported score as a random variable.** Report interval estimates (stratified bootstrap over runs within tasks) and effect sizes, and avoid "significant / not significant" verdicts. Avoid using a fixed seed as a substitute for variability: fixing the seed does not tell you how the algorithm does under other random conditions.
  - **Choose aggregate metrics before seeing results** (IQM, optimality gap, probability of improvement, and the profile plot) and show the full score distribution. Don't switch metrics after the fact; the paper shows rankings flip between median and IQM.
  - **Lock the evaluation protocol** (e.g. final-policy scores averaged over N evaluation episodes, never max-over-training) and keep it identical across compared methods. A protocol change invalidates comparison with prior numbers.
  - **Save per-run, per-task raw scores** as a data artifact so CIs and profiles can be recomputed later. The paper asks papers to release all runs.
  - **Use the lift and resampling simulation to size runs.** With 100 runs available, subsample to estimate how many runs give stable conclusions. This generalizes beyond RL to any noisy ML training.
- Caveats:
  - The paper notes that statistical sophistication can create new forms of abuse.
  - Bootstrap CIs under-cover with about 3 runs.
  - The choice of aggregate metric is still open and depends on the score distribution.
  - IQM discards tails that may matter for safety.
  - Probability of improvement ignores how large the improvement is.
  - The focus is multi-task benchmark comparison, not single-task applied RL or the validity of the claim itself. It addresses "results reproducibility", not "methods reproducibility".

Auto-memory `MEMORY.md` loads only its first 200 lines or 25 KB. Block-level HTML comments are stripped before injection, so maintainer notes cost no tokens.
  - **Verification:** `/context` lists "Memory files" loaded at launch. `/memory` lists and opens all memory files (since v2.1.280 it also shows a directly read AGENTS.md). An interactive session prints a line `no CLAUDE.md found; AGENTS.md loaded: <path>` when the fallback is used. On-demand loads print a `Loaded <path>` line. The `InstructionsLoaded` hook can log every load. `claude --debug` shows front-matter parse errors. `/doctor prompt-audit` (v2.1.283+) audits instruction files for conflicts, stale references and outdated wording without changing anything. `/doctor` proposes trims that remove derivable content.

The secondary write-ups attribute to DevOps.com the caveat that CLAUDE.local.md suppresses AGENTS.md and that some cloud or telemetry-off environments lack the feature, which matches the docs. - Implications for the method:
  - Ship the method as one canonical `AGENTS.md` for the project layer. For Claude Code, the installer should write a real (non-symlink) `CLAUDE.md` whose first line is `@AGENTS.md`, rather than a symlink and rather than relying on the fallback. The fallback silently stops working as soon as anyone adds a CLAUDE.local.md or a parent CLAUDE.md, and the symlink breaks agent self-edits and Windows clones.
  - Put personal, cross-project method rules in `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md` (Claude) and `~/.codex/AGENTS.md` (Codex), or in `~/.claude/rules/*.md`, which load without approval dialogs. Do not import files from outside the repo in project files, because that triggers approval prompts.
  - Keep each always-loaded file well under 200 lines. Move multi-step procedures (e.g. "run an experiment", "write up a result") into skills, which load on demand, and stage-specific rules into path-scoped `.claude/rules/` files (e.g. `paths: ["notebooks/**", "experiments/**"]`).
  - Write rules as concrete, checkable statements, for example "run `make test` before claiming a result" rather than "be rigorous". Anything that must always happen (e.g. blocking writes to `data/raw/`) should be a hook or a `permissions.deny`, not prose.
  - Add a verification step to the install script: tell the user to run `/context` or `/memory` (Claude) and `codex --ask-for-approval never "Summarize the current instructions."` (Codex) and check the method's text is present. Optionally add an `InstructionsLoaded` hook log.
  - Use HTML comments for maintainer notes, provenance and version stamps inside the files; Claude Code strips them so they cost nothing. Codex does not document stripping them (UNVERIFIED), so keep them short.

### S31 · Evaluating AGENTS.md: are repository-level context files helpful for coding agents? (Gloaguen, Mündler-Sasahara, Raychev, Müller, Vechev; ETH Zurich and LogicStar.ai; arXiv 2602.11988 v3, 29 Sep 2026)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11988 (v3 PDF, pdftotext) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the main text, appendices A–C, and skimmed the prompt appendix.
- Core claim: The authors ran a controlled evaluation of four agent/model pairs: Claude Code + Sonnet-4.5, Codex + GPT-5.2, Codex + GPT-5.1-mini, and Qwen Code + Qwen3-30B. Each pair ran on SWE-bench Lite (300 tasks, 11 repos, no developer context files) and on a new CTXBench (138 Python tasks from 12 niche repos that have developer-committed context files). There were three conditions: no file, an LLM-generated file (each agent's own `/init`), and the developer file. LLM-generated files did not help: average resolution changed by −0.5% on SWE-bench and −2% on CTXBench (CMH p = 0.87 and 0.37), while steps rose and cost rose significantly, by about 20–23%. Developer files gave +2.4% on average (p = 0.21, not significant) and beat LLM-generated files (p = 0.038), but still raised cost by up to 19%. For Claude Code + Sonnet-4.5 on CTXBench, the developer file *lowered* success (73.2% with none, 70.3% with the developer file, 65.2% with the LLM file). Instructions were followed. A tool mentioned in the file was used far more often (e.g. uv 1.6 times per task when mentioned versus under 0.01 when not), with more testing, exploration and reasoning tokens (+2–22%). Repository overviews did not speed up finding the relevant files. LLM files helped (+2.7%) only when all other documentation was removed. Length showed no clear relationship with success. Ablating the overview, tooling or testing sections did not change accuracy, though the testing guidance drove cost.
- Implications for the method:
  - Do not auto-generate the method's context files with `/init` and ship them as is. The evidence says this adds cost and does not add success.
  - Leave out codebase overviews and directory listings that the agent can discover itself. Include only what is non-standard and not derivable: conventions, non-functional requirements, and the scientific-method workflow rules themselves.
  - Expect every rule to be followed and to cost something: a rule saying "always run X" will make the agent run X. Each rule should earn its cost, so state the cheapest adequate check and when to run it, rather than "test everything thoroughly".
  - The method itself should include a way to evaluate its own instruction files. Run a few representative tasks with and without the file and compare outcome, steps and cost before adopting a change, as the authors recommend.
  - Data-science repos often lack good docs. The paper's documentation-removed condition suggests a short "where things are / how to run" section helps most when there is no README. Prefer writing the README for humans and keeping AGENTS.md for agent-only rules.
- Caveats: Python only, popular-language tooling, models with strong prior knowledge. The outcome measure is test-passing on bug-fix and feature tasks, not analysis validity, reproducibility or scientific rigour, which are the things this method cares about. Single samples per configuration. CTXBench task descriptions and tests are LLM-generated, and the authors say the benchmark is easier but fairer. The intro reports a "7%" developer-versus-LLM gap that does not obviously match the Results numbers (+2.4% versus −2%), so cite the section 4.2 figures. Only 12 developer files were tested, and file quality was not characterised beyond length and AI-detection scores.

### S32 · On the impact of AGENTS.md files on the efficiency of AI coding agents (Lulla, Mohsenimofidi, Galster, Zhang, Baltes, Treude; ICSE JAWs 2026; arXiv 2601.20404 v2, 30 Mar 2026)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.20404 (v2 PDF, pdftotext) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full (5-page workshop paper).
- Core claim: This is a paired within-task study using one agent (Codex CLI with gpt-5.2-codex). It covered 10 repositories, randomly drawn from 26 that have a single root AGENTS.md containing conventions, architecture and description content, with 124 small merged PRs (≤100 LoC, ≤5 files, code-only). Each PR was re-run from the pre-merge state with and without the AGENTS.md of that time. With the file, median wall-clock time fell 28.6% (98.6 s to 70.3 s; mean −20.3%) and median output tokens fell 16.6% (mean −20.1%); both were significant under a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p < 0.05). Input and total tokens did not change significantly; median total tokens moved by only about 1%. Correctness was not evaluated beyond a manual sanity check of 50 outputs for non-trivial changes. The authors speculate that the gains come from the file describing structure and conventions up front.
- Implications for the method:
  - A short, human-written root AGENTS.md with conventions and structure can make an agent faster and less verbose on small tasks. That supports having one for each project, but not that it improves correctness.
  - Read alongside S31: efficiency and success can move independently, and the result depends on the setup. The method should state which outcome it is optimising (validity of conclusions first, cost second) and measure it.
  - Favour the simple setup this study used (one root file, no conflicting nested files) as the default install, and add nested files only when there is a demonstrated need.
- Caveats: Small sample, one agent and model, small PRs only, no correctness or quality outcome, and LLM-generated task descriptions. The repos were selected for files containing architecture and description content, the content type S31 found unhelpful for success, so the two papers measure different things rather than strictly contradicting each other. Single run per condition, so agent stochasticity is uncontrolled. This is a workshop or position paper with a research roadmap.

### S33 · How many instructions can LLMs follow at once? IFScale (Jaroslawicz, Whiting, Shah, Maamari; Distyl AI; arXiv 2507.11538 v1, 15 Jul 2025)
- Source used: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.11538 (v1, the only version, pdftotext) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full. I read the main text and skimmed the appendix tables and figures.
- Core claim: IFScale asks a model to write a business report that includes N required keywords, with N from 10 to 500 in steps of 10, using 5 seeds and 20 models from 7 providers. Accuracy falls as instruction count rises. The best models reach only about 68% at 500 (o3 62.8%, claude-sonnet-4 42.9%, claude-opus-4 44.6%). Reasoning models (o3, gemini-2.5-pro) stay near-perfect up to roughly 100–250 instructions and then decline ("threshold decay"). Other models show linear decay (gpt-4.1, claude-3.7-sonnet) or exponential decay (small models, which drop to a 7–15% floor). Earlier instructions are satisfied more often (primacy bias), and this bias peaks around 150–200 instructions. Under load, errors become mostly omissions (instructions dropped entirely) rather than near-misses. Variance grows with density, and latency for reasoning models grows sharply.
- Implications for the method:
  - Keep the number of always-on rules small. Tens, not hundreds, keeps even weaker models in the near-perfect range. The whole loaded stack counts (global file, project file, rules, skill listings, and the system prompt the tool already supplies).
  - Put the most important rules (the scientific-method invariants, e.g. pre-register the hypothesis and metric, never touch held-out data, report negative results) first in each file and in the global layer. Primacy bias favours early items.
  - The expected failure is silent omission, so the method should include explicit self-checks or checklists at hand-off points and mechanical enforcement (tests, hooks) for anything critical, rather than trusting that a rule deep in a long file will be applied.
  - Push detail into on-demand skills and path-scoped rules so that density stays low at any one moment.
- Caveats: Very simple, uniform instructions (keyword inclusion) in one domain, judged by regex. Real agent rules are heterogeneous, conditional and spread across a long multi-turn agent context, so the thresholds do not transfer numerically. It is a 2025 snapshot; newer models will differ. Single task type, English only. Vendor-authored preprint with no peer review noted.

### S34 · Understanding spec-driven development: Kiro, spec-kit and Tessl (Birgitta Böckeler, Thoughtworks, on martinfowler.com, 15 Oct 2025)
- Source used: https://www.martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html (raw HTML via curl, main text extracted; screenshots not viewed) · accessed 2026-10-26 · depth: full (text).
- Core claim: "Spec-driven development" is ill-defined. The author separates three levels. *Spec-first* means writing a spec before AI coding for a task. *Spec-anchored* means keeping and evolving the spec with the feature. *Spec-as-source* means humans edit only the spec and code is generated. She also separates specs (task-scoped, behaviour-oriented natural-language artifacts) from the "memory bank" (always-relevant context such as rules files; called "steering" in Kiro and the "constitution" in spec-kit). Her hands-on trials found Kiro (requirements, design, tasks) and spec-kit (constitution, then specify, plan, tasks, with checklists) to be one-size-fits-all and heavy for small and medium changes. A small bug turned into 4 user stories with 16 acceptance criteria. spec-kit produced many repetitive Markdown files that were tedious to review. Agents still ignored instructions (e.g. re-creating existing classes) or over-applied them (e.g. a constitution article). Spec-as-source (Tessl) recalls model-driven development, with a risk of both inflexibility and non-determinism. Her conclusion is that spec-first is often valuable, but elaborate SDD tooling may make things worse.
- Implications for the method:
  - Separate the always-loaded "memory bank" (short AGENTS.md/CLAUDE.md with the method's invariants) from per-task artifacts (a short experiment or analysis spec: question, hypothesis, data, metric, success criterion, threats to validity) that live in the repo and load only when relevant.
  - Make the workflow scale with the size of the problem. A quick look or bug-fix path should not require the full hypothesis-spec-plan ceremony; provide two or three tracks.
  - Keep spec templates short and non-redundant with code or notebooks. Reviewability by the human is a design constraint, since reviewing a lot of Markdown is a real cost.
  - Do not treat checklists or "constitution" rules in prose as guarantees. Pair them with verification steps the human or tooling actually performs, and keep iterations small.
  - Aim for spec-first, optionally spec-anchored (e.g. a living per-project decision or results log). Avoid spec-as-source for analysis code.
- Caveats: These are one experienced practitioner's exploratory trials (September 2025) on a few problems, not a systematic study. The tools have changed since then. The article is software-feature oriented, not about data-science or research workflows. Screenshots were not viewed.
